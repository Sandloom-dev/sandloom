## 1. Summary

- **Today the kernel trusts names, not identities.** A claim's owner is the registering module's
  short name, an unchecked `owner=` argument, or the text of a `SERVICE_BINDINGS` key; the
  platform gates trust the string `"identity"`; `Role`, `Perm` and `TenantScope` read their keys
  with no provenance at all. PR #70 made the first writer of a key its owner for one token: an
  ordering rule, not an assignment.
- **Every precedent studied binds a claim to an authority through a verified identity (a key, a
  certificate, attested properties) that an operator maps to a name in configuration**, and lets
  later or lower sources only restrict, never grant. None trusts a source's own statement of who
  it is.
- **Recommended model** (§6): the application's composition root names, per guarded claim key,
  the module that is its authority (generated by `sandloom new`); at mint the kernel accepts a
  guarded key only from that module, identified by the import path it was loaded from (local) or
  by the pinned, MAC-verified binding of its claims contract (remote), and refuses any module
  serving another module's claims or facts contract; verifiers read guarded keys only through a
  trusted view of the subject, computed once per request from provenance and the same map, so
  `Role`/`Perm` stay zero-I/O; a provenance-policy claim in the token separates new tokens from
  legacy ones and catches a split whose services disagree on the map; a missing authority fails
  closed; a non-critical contributor's violation is its own failure, not every user's.
- **Costs**: five small mechanisms, each replacing a named violation (H-12); a per-check cost that
  the probe puts at the level of today's (31 ns against 35 ns, R6) and one dictionary lookup per
  guarded key per request; one generated argument in `asgi.py`; legacy tokens lose guarded
  claims until their next refresh (15 minutes at the default TTL). No new setting (the semantic
  ratchet stands at 15 of 16).
- **Not defended, by design**: hostile code inside a process, the holder of the token signing key,
  a compromised authority service. These match the normative trust model
  (`docs/advanced/security-model.md:548-566`).

## 2. Threat model

### 2.1 Parties and what each is trusted for

| party | example | trusted for | not trusted for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Application composition** (composition root, deploy profile, `SERVICE_BINDINGS`, keys) | `myapp/asgi.py`, `deploy/compose/*.yml` | everything it states: which modules load, which service answers which contract, which module is the authority of which claim | — (it is the root of trust; a wrong composition is the operator's error, reviewed like code) |
| **Installed in-process module code** (bundled batteries, third-party wheels, app modules) | `sandloom_billing`, `myapp.modules.inventory` | its own domain data and its own claim keys | another module's claims, the token envelope, provenance, the tenant binding. Trusted to be *buggy, not hostile* (security-model.md:529-537, :550-561) |
| **The authority of a claim** | identity for `roles`, tenancy for `tenant_ids` | the values of the keys assigned to it | other modules' keys |
| **A remote service** (split) | the tenancy service answering `tenancy.claims.v1` | what `SERVICE_BINDINGS` binds to it and `RPC_ALLOWED_CALLS` allows | anything else; a compromised service can forge calls only to itself and answer only what it is bound for (security-model.md:388-392) |
| **The signing-key holder** | the issuer process (`JWT_PRIVATE_KEY`), any process with `JWT_SECRET` | minting | — (holding the key is minting power; `tokens.py:123-176` keeps it on the issuer only under asymmetric JWT) |
| **Token holder** (end user, client) | a browser | presenting its token | its contents: it cannot change a signed token; it can replay it until expiry or revocation |
| **Verifier** (any process that serves an authenticated route) | billing service | checking signature, expiry, revocation, then gates | — |

### 2.2 What Sandloom defends (in scope)

1. **A mistaken or overreaching module cannot raise a user's rights through claims**: not by
   writing `roles`, `permissions` or `tenant_ids`, not by naming itself after the authority, not by
   passing `owner=`, not by registering the authority's claims contract, not by being registered
   first. (I-08, I-09, I-10; T1, T2, T6; validation A gap 2, B gap 1, C gap 1.)
2. **A misconfigured deployment fails closed**: no assigned authority, an assigned module that is
   loaded nowhere, a split whose services disagree on the assignment, an authority that failed at
   mint. (I-20; D2, D3.)
3. **A remote service is trusted only for the contracts the operator bound to it**, identified by
   its pinned `service_id` and the pair MAC, never by the name of what it serves.
4. **Tokens minted under an older, weaker policy do not carry privileged claims past the change**
   (C-B3).
5. **One buggy optional module does not deny every login** (validation A gap 3).

### 2.3 What Sandloom does not defend (out of scope, stated)

- **Hostile code in a process.** It can import the kernel, read `JWT_SECRET` or `JWT_PRIVATE_KEY`
  and sign any token, or forge the tenant setting (security-model.md:558-561). The checks here
  make mistakes loud and composition explicit; they are not a sandbox. Process isolation is the
  only answer and is the "future capability" of security-model.md:562-564.
- **The signing-key holder.** Any code that holds the key mints any claims and any provenance
  (guarantee-map S5; R5 below). Whether the kernel checks *who in the issuer process* mints is
  owner question Q13; §5.2.4 gives the cheap option.
- **A compromised authority.** If identity's service is compromised, its `roles` are wrong; the
  model only guarantees that nobody *else* writes them.
- **A compromised provider's X25519 key.** It allows forged reads from any caller to that provider
  (security-model.md:440-456); a forged `claims.v1` read reaches the *provider*, not the issuer, so
  it cannot mint. Not changed here.

### 2.4 Assets

`roles`, `permissions` (platform admin, `Role`, `Perm`); `tenant_ids` (TenantScope, the authz
allowlist, RLS); plan claims `subscription_status`, `plan_code`, `features`, `limits`
(`Subscribed`, `Feature`, `WithinLimit`, claim-mode facts) — their status is Q9; the reserved keys
(`_claims.py:62-91`); provenance (`claims_sources`, `claims_sources_failed`).

A token never minted by the kernel's merge passes `Role`/`Perm` with no provenance, and passes the
platform gate if it writes the string `identity` into `claims_sources`. The access token lives
`ACCESS_TOKEN_EXPIRE_MINUTES` (default 15, `core/config/auth.py:65-69`; no upper bound in the
field); the refresh token is an opaque row (`token_service.py:155-172`), and a refresh runs the
full mint again, so a new policy reaches every session at its next refresh.

### 3.4 The availability problem (R4)

A non-critical contributor (`analytics`, `critical=False`) that writes a reserved key (`jti`) or
another module's key (`roles`) refuses **every** mint with 503 `Token claims from 'analytics' are
unavailable`; writing only its own key mints normally. One optional module with a bug therefore
stops every login and refresh in the application. The refusal is right for the token (no token
with a foreign key); its scope (every token) is the problem.

## 4. Precedents

Each source was opened for this task (links in §9). What each does, and what it teaches here.

**OAuth/JWT: RFC 8725 (JWT BCP).** §3.8: when a JWT has an `iss`, the application must confirm
that the verification keys belong to that issuer; how it learns an issuer's keys is up to it.
§3.12: when one issuer issues several kinds of JWT, validation rules must be mutually exclusive
(typing, required claims, keys, audiences). *Teaches:* the name (`iss`) means something only
because a key is bound to it out of band; Sandloom's `claims_sources` names have no key behind
them. A provenance-policy marker in the token is a §3.12-style "different required claim" that
separates new tokens from legacy ones.

**OpenID Connect Core.** §3.1.3.7: the issuer identifier "MUST exactly match" the `iss` claim, and
the client must be in `aud`. §5.6.2, aggregated claims: claims from a Claims Provider other than
the OP travel as a JWT *signed by that provider*, referenced by `_claim_names` /
`_claim_sources`, with `iss` so the relying party can fetch the provider's keys. *Teaches:* the
standard answer to "claims from another authority inside my token" is a per-source signature.
It is the strongest option for remote providers (§5.2, b-3) and the most expensive.

**RFC 8693 (Token Exchange).** The STS validates the presented tokens and decides what to issue;
`scope`, `resource` and `audience` in the request are inputs; `may_act` "is authorized to become
the actor", an input to the server's decision, not a grant; nested `act` entries are
informational and not used for access control. *Teaches:* the issuer applies *its* policy to what
contributors return; a contributor's statement is input, never authority. That is the role of the
kernel's merge.

**ASP.NET Core claims.** `IClaimsTransformation` runs after successful authentication, may run
several times per request, and only the last registered implementation is used ("last-registration
-wins"). `Claim.Issuer` is "a name ... taken from a list of well-known issuers maintained by the
issuer name registry", which "associates a mnemonic name ... with the cryptographic material".
*Teaches:* two things. Claim transformation by ordered, unowned code is the anti-pattern PR #70
partly fixed (last-wins was Sandloom's pre-#70 merge). And the issuer *name* on a claim is the
output of a registry that maps verified key material to names; Sandloom needs the same registry,
with the module's load path (local) or the binding's pinned service (remote) as the "material".

**ABP.** `IAbpClaimsPrincipalContributor` adds claims at authentication; the docs state no
ordering, ownership or override rule. Dynamic claims: only claim types listed in the
`DynamicClaims` option are overridden by the dynamic system. *Teaches:* contributor frameworks
without ownership leave the problem to the application; ABP's one control is an application-side
allowlist of which claim types may be rewritten. That is the shape of an application-assigned map.

**Kubernetes authentication.** The authenticating-proxy mode trusts `X-Remote-User`-style headers
only after the proxy's client certificate validates against `--requestheader-client-ca-file`, and
only for CNs in `--requestheader-allowed-names`; the docs warn against reusing a CA that serves
another purpose. Webhook token authentication is configured by the cluster administrator in a
kubeconfig-format file naming the remote service and the API server's client credentials; the
`TokenReview` answer supplies `username`, `groups`, `extra`. Server-assigned names live under
`system:`; the docs' example configuration rejects users and groups with that prefix through
`userValidationRules`, and notes that prefixing OIDC names prevents clashes. *Teaches:* the
closest analogue to a remote claims contributor: the header (claim) is trusted only from an
identity the operator listed, proven by a certificate (for Sandloom: the pinned `service_id` and
the pair MAC). Reserved name spaces are refused at the boundary, as PR #70 does for reserved keys.

**Kubernetes RBAC.** A user may create or update a role only if it already holds every permission
in it, or has `escalate`; a binding only with the role's permissions or `bind`. Bootstrap needs a
`system:masters` credential. Default roles are reconciled at every start. Migration from ABAC: run
both authorizers in parallel, log RBAC denials, grant what is missing, then remove ABAC; a
permissive `cluster-admin` binding is "not a recommended policy". *Teaches:* (1) no party grants
what it does not hold: a contributor cannot write a right it was not assigned; (2) a stricter
policy is rolled out by observing denials first, then enforcing.

**Kubernetes legacy service-account tokens.** Using a legacy token emits a warning, a metric, and a
`kubernetes.io/legacy-token-last-used` label; tokens unused for a configured period (default one
year) are labelled `kubernetes.io/legacy-token-invalid-since` and then rejected; removing the
label re-activates; unused ones are later deleted. *Teaches:* credentials minted under an old
policy are first made visible, then refused, with a documented way back. Sandloom's tokens live
15 minutes, so the same idea collapses to "refuse guarded claims of legacy tokens; the client
refreshes".

**SPIFFE / SPIRE.** A SPIFFE ID names a workload in a trust domain; an SVID (X.509 or JWT) signed by
the domain's authority proves it, checked against the trust bundle. The operator creates
registration entries mapping a SPIFFE ID to selectors (Unix uid, path, Kubernetes service
account); the agent attests the calling process through the OS and the platform and returns the
SVID only when the selectors match: identity "relies on attested properties rather than claims".
*Teaches:* the identity of a writer is established by something the writer cannot choose (for a
local module: the import path the composition root loaded it from; for a remote one: the service
key), and the operator's registration maps it to a name. A module's own `owner=` is a "claim"
in SPIRE's sense and must not count.

**Envoy ext_authz.** `failure_mode_allow` defaults to false: an unreachable or failing authorization
service means Forbidden. Which response headers the authorization service may copy onto the
upstream request is an operator allowlist (`allowed_upstream_headers`), and
`decoder_header_mutation_rules` limit what it may set or remove; it can never remove `:`-prefixed
headers. *Teaches:* an external decision source writes only the keys the operator allowed it, and
its absence denies. That is the per-key authority map plus fail-closed absence.

**Biscuit and macaroons.** Macaroons' caveats "attenuate and contextually confine" a bearer
credential. In Biscuit, rules and the authorizer trust facts from the authority block and the
authorizer by default; facts from later blocks are not trusted, so "adding a block can only
restrict what a token can do"; `trusting {public_key}` admits facts from a third-party block
signed by that key. *Teaches:* the cleanest model of claim provenance: facts carry their block,
trust is declared per verifier rule by key, and a block can be signed by its own authority. It is
the design Sandloom would adopt if contributors were mutually untrusted processes; for in-process
modules the per-block signatures buy nothing a key-holding issuer could not forge (§2.3).

**Keycloak.** Protocol mappers are configured by the realm administrator per client or client
scope and decide what enters the token. A known vulnerability class is a user-editable attribute
that a mapper puts into tokens; Keycloak's user profile lets an administrator make an attribute
admin-edit only, and CVE-2026-0871 was a bypass of that restriction. (The admin guide was too long
to open in full; the mapper and attribute points rest on the guide's core-concepts section and on
secondary sources, listed in §9.) *Teaches:* the party that controls a token claim's *data* must be
the one the administrator assigned; a claim mapped from data some other party can write is an
escalation path, even when the mapper itself is administrator-configured.

### 4.1 What they agree on

1. **A name is bound to a verified identity by an operator-controlled registry** (RFC 8725 §3.8,
   `IssuerNameRegistry`, `requestheader-allowed-names`, SPIRE registration entries, Biscuit's
   `trusting {key}`). Self-description never counts.
2. **The assignment of who may supply which claim is configuration the operator owns** (Kubernetes
   flags and files, Envoy's allowlists, Keycloak mappers, ABP's `DynamicClaims`).
3. **Later or lower sources only restrict** (Biscuit blocks, macaroon caveats, RBAC escalation
   rules, RFC 8693's `may_act`).
4. **Absence or failure denies** (Envoy `failure_mode_allow=false`, Kubernetes webhook deny,
   OIDC's "must be prepared for claims not returned").
5. **A stricter policy is rolled out by observing, then enforcing, with an explicit legacy marker**
   (ABAC→RBAC parallel authorizers, legacy-token labels, RFC 8725 §3.12 explicit typing).

Why the composition root and not a setting: the composition root is code that passes admission
(review, `sandloom check`) and is the same file in every service of a split
(`SANDLOOM_COMPOSITION_ROOT=<app>.asgi` for every service: `core/cli/project.py:875`,
`core/cli/scaffold_deploy.py:185`). A trust assignment changes as rarely as the module set and
belongs with it. The deploy-time part (which service hosts the authority) is already
`SERVICE_BINDINGS`.

Which keys need an authority (Q9). The model does not need a kernel list of module keys: the rule
is "a kernel gate reads a claim only if the composition assigned that key". `roles`,
`permissions`, `tenant_ids` are kernel concepts (I-21) and always guarded; `subscription_status`,
`plan_code`, `features`, `limits` become guarded the moment an atom reads them, which today
`Subscribed`, `Feature`, `WithinLimit` do (`atoms_claims.py:180-188`, `:304-363`). The generated map
assigns them to billing; an app without billing has no assignment and those atoms deny (D2 closed
without naming billing in the kernel).

#### 5.2.3 What b-l1 + b-r1 + b-r2 give

A guarded key reaches a token only from the module the composition assigned, loaded from the path
it named, or from the service the operator bound to that module's claims contract, whose answer is
MAC-bound to that service and whose own process refuses to let another module answer under that
name. A module naming itself `identity`, passing `owner="identity"`, registering
`identity.claims.v1` or being registered first gets nothing.

mode stays as it is: the binding is the trust, the owner name is not used.

## 6. Recommended model

### 6.1 In plain words

The application's composition root says which module is the authority of each claim that a
security gate reads; `sandloom new` writes that list for the bundled modules. When a token is
minted, the kernel accepts such a claim only from that module: locally, from the module the
composition root loaded from that path; remotely, from the service the operator bound to that
module's claims contract, which is pinned and MAC-verified, and whose own process refuses to let
another module answer under that name. The kernel alone stamps provenance and a policy marker
into the token. Every verifier reads those claims only through a view of the subject that it
computes once per request from the provenance, the marker and the same list, with no I/O. A claim
with no assigned authority, or whose authority did not vouch for it, is absent: the gate denies.
A token minted before the change has no marker, keeps working for ordinary routes, and loses its
privileged claims until the client refreshes. A non-critical module that writes what it does not
own loses its own contribution, not everybody's login.

### 6.2 Mechanisms (H-12: what each removes)

| # | mechanism | replaces / removes | serves |
|---|---|---|---|
| M1 | `claim_authorities` in `create_app`, generated by `sandloom new`, passed by `sandloom.main`; startup checks e-2, e-3, e-4 | trust by the name `identity` (`platform.py:16`, `admin/api/platform.py:67`); PR #70's first writer as owner | T1, T2, T3 (scope Q9), T6; I-08 assignment, I-22 |
| M2 | the merge accepts a guarded key only from its authority's step (module path locally, binding remotely); `owner=` refused when it differs from the registering module; violations by non-critical writers per f-2 | `_refuse_foreign_claim_keys` first-writer rule (extended, not added: same pass); `owner=` spoofing | I-08 verification; A-F2, A-F3, A-B3, C-F4; validation A gaps 2-3, B gap 1 |
| M3 | contract-name ownership for `<m>.claims.v1`, `<m>.facts.v1` and `AccessFact.owner_module` | `register_service` / `register_access_fact` with any name | T5 (for these two families), T7, C4, R2 |
| M4 | trusted subject view in `UserContext`; one platform-admission function shared by `require_platform_admin` and `platform_operator`; claim-mode fact trust by the claim key's authority | `Role`/`Perm`/`TenantScope`/allowlist reads without provenance; two copies of the platform gate | I-09, I-10, D2, D3; validation A gap 1 |
| M5 | `cpv` (policy version + map digest) stamped by the merge, required by M4; `create_access_token` refuses provenance, `cpv` and guarded keys outside a sealed merge result (m-2) | legacy-token trust (§3.3), S5 for mistakes, split skew | C-B3, T4 (partly, Q13) |

root, M2 the existing merge pass, M3 the existing registration path, M4 the existing `UserContext`
construction, M5 the existing mint.

### 6.3 Costs

| dimension | cost |
|---|---|
| hot path | per check ≈ today (R6: 31 ns against 35 ns for the role test); per request: one string compare (`cpv`) and, per guarded key, one dict lookup and two set tests at `UserContext` construction; to be confirmed by cachegrind on the request rung (ADR-0010's +1 % budget; P-12) |
| mint | one comparison per contributed key (the merge already loops over keys, `_claims.py:516`); one `cpv` claim (≈ 25 bytes) |
| DX, app | one generated argument in `asgi.py`; replacing identity edits one value; a typo is a startup refusal with a code |
| DX, module author | nothing unless the module writes a guarded key; with M6, one tuple in `define_module` |
| migration | every token minted before the release loses guarded claims until its refresh (≤ 15 min by default; d-3 makes it one request); apps not built by `sandloom new` add the argument or get fail-closed gates (startup warning in the issuer process naming the guarded keys without an authority); a third-party issuer must mint through `collect_claims` (m-2) |
| public API | `create_app(claim_authorities=...)`, possibly `define_module(claims=...)`; `owner=` leaves the facade (L3, not in `public_api_baseline.json`'s module surface — to confirm); upgrade steps per `docs/contributing/versioning.md` |
| tests | one test per T-row in scope (I-22 missing proof) and the PoC set in §7 |

### 6.4 Trade-offs against the strongest alternatives

- **Code argument (a-2) vs setting (a-3).** a-2 keeps the decision in reviewed code and identical
  across services; a-3 lets an operator re-assign without a release and costs the last semantic
  slot. If operators must re-assign per deployment (Q-T1), a-3 becomes necessary; then `cpv`
  catches skew at runtime and a `SHARED_KEYS` entry at test time.
- **Binding as remote identity (b-r1) vs signed claims (b-r3).** b-r1 adds nothing and trusts the
  issuer to stamp honestly; b-r3 protects against a hostile issuer at the price of keys,
  signatures and token size. Since the issuer holds the signing key anyway, b-r3's gain is small
  in Sandloom's trust model.
- **Trusted view (c-3) vs mint-only (c-1).** c-1 costs nothing and is enough if every token comes
  from the kernel's merge under the current policy; c-3 costs about nothing per check and covers
  legacy, hand-minted and skewed tokens. The probe shows c-3's per-check cost is not higher.
- **f-2 vs I-08 as written.** f-2 trades "no token" for "no token *with the violator's claims*"
  when the violator is non-critical. Rights cannot rise either way; availability differs.

### 6.5 Interactions

- **`kb-research-authz-portability`**: M4 changes where `Role`/`Perm`/`Subscribed` read claims, not
  their syntax, call counts or evaluation order (I-23 holds). The kernel defaults
  `claim_key="subscription_status"` and `ACTIVE_SUBSCRIPTION_STATUSES` (`atoms_claims.py:32`,
  `:118`) are billing's vocabulary in the kernel; that is portability work, not trust work.
- **`kb-research-settings`**: the model adds no setting (a-2). If Q-T1 chooses a-3, it needs a
  semantic slot.
- **D1, T8 (revocation applier)** and **T9 (operator contract)** are separate trust decisions; M1's
  shape ("the composition names the module that fulfils a kernel duty") could carry them, which is
  the owner's call under Q12.
- **Email claims (Q9 second half)**: `email`, `email_verified` are written after the merge by
  identity (`token_service.py:146-151`); under the model they are ordinary keys with identity as
  authority if the map lists them, and the mint-order protection becomes a kernel check.
